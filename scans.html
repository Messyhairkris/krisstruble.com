Field Scans

From replacement parts to digital exhibits.

My interest in 3D scanning started while I was running Near Future, a maker education project where I taught 3D printing and CAD. I was looking for better ways to repair damaged objects.

One repair method I taught there was rebuilding a broken or missing part from a photograph: bring the photo into Fusion 360, set it to the correct scale, and use it as a reference to design the replacement. It worked surprisingly well. But a photograph is flat, and I kept thinking about what would happen if the reference itself could be captured in 3D.

I experimented with photogrammetry, then with a hacked Kinect and Skanect. The results were not accurate or useful enough for the repairs I had in mind, but the idea stuck.

Years later, phones had become remarkably good at capturing 3D objects. The technology had finally reached the point where I could make attractive captures with something as ordinary as an iPhone.

But there was a catch.

A scan is not necessarily a model

The newer capture methods I have been working with, particularly Gaussian splats, are excellent for capturing the appearance of an object. They are not solid models, though, and they cannot simply be brought into a CAD program and used to build a replacement part.

For the original repair problem, that makes a splat more useful as a proportional visual reference than as something I can directly design from.

That limitation pushed my interest in a different direction: if I could not easily turn these captures into useful CAD geometry, what else could I do with them?

Making scans people can actually explore

That question led to Splat Portfolio, a browser-based tool I built to make captured objects easier to clean up, publish, and explore.

The raw captures were surprisingly large and often contained floating specks, haze, and other artifacts. I wanted a way to take a capture, prepare it for the web, and give someone a simple way to look around it without installing specialized software.

I built it with Claude Code and kept iterating until it handled the practical parts of publishing a scan: cleaning it up, reducing its size, setting up a camera tour, adding notes, and producing a web-ready result. The viewer at the top of this page is the result, and it runs entirely in the browser.

What I want to do with it

This is where the project became more interesting to me than the original repair problem.

A physical object exists in one place. A digital capture can be shared with people who could never visit it, handle it, or even know that it exists.

I can see this being useful for digital museum exhibits, documenting events, recording places at a particular moment, or creating an archive of objects and experiences that might otherwise disappear.

The scan does not have to replace the original. It can give people another way to encounter it.

Still experimenting

The project started with a practical question about repairing physical objects and ended up somewhere I did not expect.

I am still interested in the original problem. A good 3D model that could provide accurate geometry for CAD would be incredibly useful. But until capture technology gets there, I have found another reason to keep scanning: making physical things available to people who cannot be there to see them.

That makes Field Scans an ongoing experiment in both capture and presentation, and a chance to explore what becomes possible when ordinary devices can record increasingly rich versions of the physical world.
